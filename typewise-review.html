One undefined word decides your invoice

There is one number and no plan table: 'Starting from $1 per resolution', stated on the home page beside a free trial, a demo booking and an ROI calculator. There is no pricing page at all — /pricing returns a 404 and the sitemap lists none — so every rate above that floor is quote-based (2026-08-17, read on their own site). Because the bill scales with resolutions rather than seats, one unpublished definition decides your invoice, and it is the word 'resolution'.

Get these three answers in writing before the trial, not after: does a reopened ticket count as a second resolution, does a conversation handed over to a human still bill, and what is the actual rate at your volume? $1 is a floor rather than a rate card. The middle question is the one that can invert the business case. If a handover still counts, then every conversation the AI cannot finish costs you twice — once to Typewise and once to the agent who picks it up — and the tool becomes more expensive precisely on the tickets it handles worst.

If a handover does not bill, the pricing is aligned with your interests and the risk largely disappears. Nothing else about this purchase matters as much as that one clause. Then price it against the right comparison. Per-resolution pricing is not priced against other software, it is priced against labour: at $1 a resolution, a team handling 2,000 tickets a month is looking at up to $2,000 a month, or $24,000 a year, if everything resolves through the platform.

Work out your own fully-loaded cost per human-resolved ticket — agent salary and overhead divided by tickets closed — and set $1 beside it. If it is a tenth of that, the case survives almost any deflection rate; if it is half, the deflection rate has to be very high before this saves anything. The ROI calculator on their site is the vendor's own arithmetic run on the vendor's own assumptions.

It is a reasonable place to start a conversation and a poor place to end one. The pilot is where you replace it with yours: measure the deflection rate on your real ticket mix, count the handovers, and check what each of those did to the invoice.

How to actually use Typewise

Ask what a resolution is, in writing, before you start the trial. The whole bill hangs on that one word and it is published nowhere. Three specific questions: does a reopened ticket bill twice, does a handover to a human still count as a resolution, and what is the rate at your monthly volume? '$1' is a floor, not a rate card, and there is no pricing page to check it against.

Work out your own cost per ticket before the demo. Take your support team's fully-loaded cost and divide by tickets closed; that figure, not a software comparison, is what per-resolution pricing has to beat. It also gives you something concrete to negotiate with. Then run the pilot on your real mix rather than your simple tickets. Measure two things: what share of conversations the AI closed without a human, and what each handover did to the invoice.

The vendor's ROI calculator is their arithmetic on their assumptions — the pilot exists to replace it with yours.

The natural comparison is Intercom Fin or your helpdesk's native AI — the honest comparison is where the work happens: Fin lives inside Intercom's own desk, while Typewise sells itself as a layer above the stack you already run, reading and writing in CRM, ERP and commerce systems. Already on Intercom? The native option starts ahead. Support work spread across several systems? That layer is the argument — ask both what counts as a resolution before you compare the price.

What should I weigh before buying Operations & Workflow software?

Usually it sits on top rather than replacing everything. Tools in this class tend to become the layer where work is tracked and handed off, while your existing systems stay the source of truth for their own data. Before you buy, map which tools it would replace outright and which it would merely sync with. If the answer is that it syncs with all of them, you are adding a layer rather than removing one.
